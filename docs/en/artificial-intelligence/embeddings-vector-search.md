# Embeddings and vector search

Mini-project MP-AI-3, in [`projects/artificial-intelligence/embeddings-vector-search`](../../../projects/artificial-intelligence/embeddings-vector-search). It teaches how meaning becomes a vector and how similar vectors are found. The background is section 8 of [the area page](README.md#8-embeddings-and-similarity), with the dot product and the cosine of [section 3](README.md#3-vectors-and-matrices) and the retrieval step of [section 11](README.md#11-using-llms).

## The problem

A program compares numbers, not meanings. To ask "which text is about the same thing as this question?", each word and each text must first become a list of numbers, a **vector**, built so that similar meanings give similar vectors. Then two more problems appear: how to measure "similar", and how to find the most similar vector among many without looking at all of them.

The project does the three steps with counting and arithmetic only. There is no neural network and nothing is trained.

## Step 1: a word is known by the company it keeps

The **distributional hypothesis** says that words used in the same contexts have similar meanings. So, for every word, count the words that appear near it. Take a corpus of four sentences and call "near" anything in the same sentence:

```text
the dog eats     the cat eats     the car stops     the bus stops
```

The **co-occurrence table** has one row and one column per word. Row "dog" says how often each other word appeared near "dog":

```text
          bus  car  cat  dog  eats  stops  the
dog        0    0    0    0    1     0      1
cat        0    0    0    0    1     0      1
car        0    0    0    0    0     1      1
the        1    1    1    1    2     2      0
```

Each row is the vector of its word. "dog" and "cat" have the same row, although they never appear in the same sentence: they are similar because they keep the same company. That is the whole idea of an embedding.

In the project, "near" is a window of 4 words on each side, inside one sentence, and the corpus has 2492 sentences and 760 distinct words, so each word vector has 760 numbers.

## Step 2: measuring similarity with the cosine

The **dot product** of two vectors multiplies them position by position and adds the results. The **norm** is the length of a vector: the square root of its dot product with itself. The **cosine similarity** is the dot product divided by the two norms:

```text
cosine(a, b) = dot(a, b) / (norm(a) * norm(b))
```

It measures the angle between the vectors and ignores their length: 1 means the same direction, 0 means nothing in common. For "dog" and "car" above:

```text
dot(dog, car)  = 1*1 (the column "the") + 0 everywhere else = 1
norm(dog)      = sqrt(1*1 + 1*1) = 1.414        norm(car) = 1.414
cosine         = 1 / (1.414 * 1.414) = 0.5
```

Half similar, and the only thing they share is "the". That is a problem.

If every vector is first divided by its own norm, all norms are 1 and the cosine is just the dot product. The project stores every vector that way.

## Step 3: why raw counts are not enough, and PPMI

"the" is near almost every word. In a real table its column holds the biggest number of nearly every row, so all rows point in almost the same direction and every word looks like every other word.

**PMI** (pointwise mutual information) asks a better question: how many times more often do these two words meet than they would by chance, given how frequent each one is?

```text
pmi(w, c) = log2( count(w, c) * total / (row sum of w * row sum of c) )
```

In the small table the total of all counts is 24, the row of "dog" adds up to 2, the row of "the" to 8 and the row of "eats" to 4:

```text
pmi(dog, the)  = log2(1 * 24 / (2 * 8)) = log2(1.5) = 0.585    no surprise
pmi(dog, eats) = log2(1 * 24 / (2 * 4)) = log2(3)   = 1.585    typical of "dog"
```

**PPMI** keeps the positive values and writes 0 for the rest (a pair seen less often than chance, or never). With the PPMI rows, the cosine of "dog" and "car" falls from 0.5 to 0.12, and the cosine of "dog" and "cat" stays 1. Both numbers are checked by the tests.

## The corpus

The corpus is generated by `ts/src/generate-corpus.ts` with a fixed seed, so no third-party text is used and the file can be rebuilt byte for byte. There are 8 groups of 10 words (animals, foods, vehicles, colours, weather, instruments, devices, professions). Each group has its own sentence templates and its own context words:

```text
the rabbit and the goat slept behind the meadow
the warm bread smelled good in the kitchen
the driver parked the bus beside the train
he mixed black and pink paint on the palette
```

One sentence in ten mixes two groups ("a black deer stood beside the tram"), because real text is not tidy. The 30 hand-written passages of the retrieval demo are added to the corpus, so that their words have vectors too.

The code that builds the vectors never sees the groups. Recovering them is the test.

## Finding the nearest vector: brute force

**Brute force** compares the query with every stored vector and keeps the best. It is always right, and it costs one dot product per stored vector: n comparisons for n vectors.

In TypeScript that is a loop. In Python with NumPy it is one line, `vectors @ query`: a matrix with one stored vector per row, multiplied by the query, gives all the similarities at once. This is what a vector database does when it runs an exact search.

## Finding the nearest vector: an index of random planes

An **approximate index** looks only at a promising part of the data. The one built here is random-hyperplane LSH (locality-sensitive hashing).

Draw a random plane through the origin. Every vector falls on one side or the other, and the side is the sign of the dot product of the vector with the direction perpendicular to the plane. The useful fact is:

```text
chance that two vectors fall on the same side = 1 - (angle between them) / 180 degrees
```

Vectors that point in nearly the same direction are rarely separated. Now draw several planes and write the sides as bits. Each pattern of bits is a **bucket**:

```text
3 planes:   vector a -> above, below, above -> 101 -> bucket 5
            vector b -> above, below, above -> 101 -> bucket 5   (a and b are similar)
            vector c -> below, above, below -> 010 -> bucket 2
```

To search, compute the bucket of the query and compare it only with the vectors of that bucket.

There are three knobs:

- **Bits** (planes per table). More bits give more and smaller buckets: fewer comparisons, and a higher chance that the true neighbour is cut away by one of the planes.
- **Tables.** One table can miss, so the index keeps several, each with its own planes, and joins their candidates: fewer misses, more comparisons.
- **Probing.** The most likely miss is a neighbour that differs in exactly one bit. Flipping one bit of the key at a time visits those buckets too.

A calculation shows why one table is not enough. A neighbour at cosine 0.97 is 14 degrees away, so one plane keeps the pair together with probability 1 - 14/180 = 0.92. Twelve planes all keep it together with probability 0.92 to the power 12, about 0.38. With four independent tables the chance that at least one of them works is 1 - (1 - 0.38) to the power 4, about 0.85.

## Result 2: agreement against comparisons

What was indexed: the 1927 sentences of the corpus with distinct content words, each turned into one vector of 760 numbers (as in step "Retrieval" below). The 80 word vectors and the 30 passages alone would be too few for an index to matter. The queries are 400 new sentences from another seed, none of them in the corpus. For each query, brute force gives the true nearest sentence, and the index is right when its top result is that same sentence.

| index | 8 | 10 | no | 97.3% | 117.1 | 80 | 197.1 | 10.2% |
| index | 2 | 10 | 1 bit | 94.3% | 113.1 | 20 | 133.1 | 6.9% |
| **index (chosen)** | 4 | 12 | 1 bit | 98.5% | 172.5 | 48 | 220.5 | 11.4% |
| index | 8 | 12 | 1 bit | 100.0% | 250.1 | 96 | 346.1 | 18.0% |

How to read it:

1. One table of 12 bits compares the query with about 10 vectors out of 1927 and finds the true neighbour only 37.0% of the time. Speed was paid with wrong answers.
2. More tables raise the agreement and the cost together: 37.0%, 82.8%, 94.3% for 1, 4 and 8 tables. The 82.8% of four tables is close to the 0.85 of the calculation above.
3. Fewer bits (10 in place of 12) make bigger buckets: 97.3% in place of 94.3%, with more vectors compared.
4. Probing the neighbouring buckets is the cheapest way up: 4 tables with probing reach 98.5% with 11.4% of the work of brute force. That is the chosen setting, and the test of MP-AI-3.2 asserts that it stays at or above 95%.
5. The column "Plane dot products" is the cost of computing the bucket keys of the query (tables times bits). It is part of the work, so it is counted in the total.

The share of right answers of an approximate index is its **recall**. Every real vector index is a point on a curve like this one, and choosing the point is an engineering decision.

## Retrieval: the question picks the passages

A whole text becomes one vector by adding the vectors of its words and normalising the sum:

```text
vector(text) = normalise( sum over the words of idf(word) * vector(word) )
idf(word)    = ln( number of sentences / sentences that contain the word )
```

The **idf** weight makes a rare word count more than a common one. A short stop list removes function words ("the", "how", "why"), which a corpus this small cannot recognise by counting. A word that is not in the vocabulary has no vector and is ignored.

The 30 passages are embedded once. A question is embedded in the same way, compared with the 30 passage vectors by brute force, and the 3 best are printed:

```text
question: "Which animal guards the farm at night?"
words used: guards farm night
not in the vocabulary: animal
compared with 30 passages by brute force

1. score 0.666  p01  The farm dog
   A farm dog sleeps lightly beside the barn. At night it guards the yard and barks when a fox comes near the hens. In the morning the farmer rewards it with a bone.
2. score 0.315  p08  Night trains
   A night train crosses the country while its passengers sleep in narrow beds. It stops at small stations in the dark, and the engine is changed at the border before sunrise.
3. score 0.282  p15  The first snow
   The first snow of winter usually falls at night and melts by noon. Real cold comes later, when frost hardens the ground and the snow stays on the hills for weeks.
```

This is the retrieval step of **RAG** (retrieval-augmented generation): the passages found here are what would be pasted into the prompt of a language model, so that it answers from them. The project stops at the retrieval. No language model is called.

## The same result in two languages

TypeScript is the reference implementation, with every dot product written as a loop. Python is here because NumPy changes how the code reads: the co-occurrence table is a matrix, PPMI is one expression over the whole matrix, and a search is `vectors @ query`.

Both must write the same tables, and three details make that possible:

- The vocabulary is sorted, so each word has the same row number in both.
- The random planes come from a small seeded generator (mulberry32) written by hand in both languages, using integer arithmetic only. The built-in generators of the two languages can never agree.
- The index stores each distinct content once. Two sentences with the same content words in another order have the same vector, and a tie between two copies would be decided by the last digit of a sum.

The tests of both languages compare their output with `data/expected.json`, and the two results files are identical apart from the title and the command that generated them.

## What a real system adds

- **Learned, dense embeddings.** A trained model maps a word or a whole text to a few hundred numbers, none of them tied to a specific word. Similar meanings land close together even when the wording differs ("rise" and "rises", "car" and "automobile"), and the vector of a word can depend on the sentence around it.
- **Sub-word tokens.** With the tokens of [bpe-tokenizer](bpe-tokenizer.md) there is no unknown word: an unseen word is split into known pieces.
- **Better indexes.** Graph indexes (HNSW) and inverted files with quantisation reach high recall with far fewer comparisons than random planes, on millions of vectors.
- **Chunking.** Long documents are cut into passages before being embedded, and the size of the pieces changes what is found.
- **Hybrid search and reranking.** Many systems combine vector search with keyword search and then reorder the best candidates with a slower, more precise model.
- **The generation step.** In RAG the retrieved passages go into the prompt of a language model. The answer can only be as good as what was retrieved.
