# Tiny language model

Mini-project MP-AI-4, in [`projects/artificial-intelligence/tiny-language-model`](../../../projects/artificial-intelligence/tiny-language-model). It teaches how a language model predicts the next token, from counting to self-attention. The background is in sections 9 and 10 of the area page: [attention and the transformer](README.md#9-attention-and-the-transformer) and [how a language model predicts and samples](README.md#10-how-a-language-model-predicts-and-samples).

## The problem

A language model does one thing: given the text so far, it gives a probability to every possible next token. Writing text is a loop around that: ask for the probabilities, choose one token, append it, ask again.

This project builds two such models on the same text and compares them:

1. a **bigram table**, made by counting, which looks only at the previous token;
2. a small **transformer**, which looks at up to 32 previous tokens through self-attention.

Both are written with Python and NumPy. There is no PyTorch and no TensorFlow, so every step, including the gradient of attention, can be read in the code.

A token here is one character. Cutting text into bigger tokens is another subject, shown in [bpe-tokenizer](bpe-tokenizer.md).

## A text where the context matters

If the next character depended only on the previous one, a table would be enough and attention would have nothing to add. So the text is generated by a small grammar with four kinds of line, each hiding a clue several characters back:

| Kind | Example | The clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pronoun | `ana has a cat. she likes it.` | "she" or "he" depends on the name, 15 characters back |
| agreement | `the red cats see a dog.` | one cat "sees", two cats "see": the verb depends on the "s" of the subject |
| arithmetic | `tom says 4+5=9.` | the digit after "=" depends on both numbers |
| brackets | `([x]y){z}` | the closing bracket must match the last one left open |

The generator has a fixed seed and produces 2400 different lines with a vocabulary of 45 characters. A line is kept only the first time it is drawn, so no line appears twice. The lines are shuffled, 2160 are used for training and the last 240 are **held out**: no model ever trains on them, and a test checks that the two sets share no line. The held-out lines are new combinations of known words. The model saw "ana", "cat" and "she" during training, but never that exact sentence.

Measuring on held-out text is what separates learning a rule from memorising the training lines.

## Step 1: the bigram, a table of counts

Count how many times each character follows each other one, then divide every row by its total:

```text
after "the":  cat 3 times, dog 1 time
P(cat | the) = 3/4 = 0.75      P(dog | the) = 1/4 = 0.25
```

Each row is now a probability distribution: its numbers are between 0 and 1 and add up to 1. That is the whole model. There is no training loop.

One detail matters. A pair that never appeared in training would get probability 0, and one such pair in the held-out text would make the loss infinite. So 1 is added to every cell before dividing (**add-one smoothing**). With three possible tokens the example becomes (3 + 1) / (4 + 3) = 0.571 for "cat", and the unseen "the the" gets 1/7 and not 0.

To write text, start from a character, draw the next one from its row, and repeat. The draw uses a random generator with a seed, so the same seed always writes the same text.

## How good is a prediction: cross-entropy and perplexity

For each character of the text, look at the probability the model gave to the character that really came, and take minus its natural logarithm. The **cross-entropy** is the mean:

```text
the model gave 0.5, 0.25 and 1.0 to the three characters that really came
loss = (-ln 0.5 - ln 0.25 - ln 1.0) / 3 = (0.693 + 1.386 + 0) / 3 = 0.693
```

Giving probability 1 to the truth costs 0. Being surprised costs a lot. The unit is the nat, because the logarithm is the natural one.

The **perplexity** is exp(loss). Here exp(0.693) = 2: on average this model hesitates as if it had to choose between 2 equally likely characters. A model that knows nothing and gives 1/45 to each of the 45 characters has loss ln(45) = 3.807 and perplexity 45.

## Step 2: the transformer

```text
character ids
   |
[ token embedding + position embedding ]     one vector of 48 numbers per character
   |
[ block 1: attention -> MLP ]                characters exchange information, then each one "thinks"
[ block 2: attention -> MLP ]
   |
[ final linear layer + softmax ]
   |
a probability for each of the 45 characters, at every position
```

The pieces, in the order of `python/transformer.py`:

- **Embeddings.** Each character id picks a row of a table of learned vectors. Attention by itself sees a bag of tokens with no order, so a second learned vector, chosen by the position, is added. A test zeroes the position vectors and shows that "1 2 3" and "2 1 3" then give the same prediction.
- **Self-attention.** Each position produces a query ("what am I looking for?"), a key ("what do I offer?") and a value ("what I pass on if chosen"). The score between two positions is the dot product of a query and a key, divided by the square root of the vector size. Softmax turns the scores of a row into weights that add up to 1, and the output is the average of the values with those weights.
- **Causal mask.** A position must not look at what comes after it, or it would copy the answer. Before softmax the scores of later positions are set to minus infinity, so their weight is exactly 0.
- **Four heads.** The 48 numbers are cut into 4 slices of 12, and each slice runs its own attention.
- **MLP.** After attention, each position goes alone through a small network with ReLU.
- **Residual connections and layer normalisation.** Each of the two steps adds its result to its input (x = x + f(x)) and normalises the input first. This "pre-norm" layout is the one used by GPT-2.

The model has 62 253 numbers to learn.

### Backpropagation by hand, and how to know it is right

Training needs, for every one of those numbers, the direction in which the loss falls: the gradient. A framework computes it automatically. Here it is written by hand, walking the forward pass backwards with the chain rule. Two rules cover almost all of it:

```text
y = x @ W          ->   dW = x^T @ dy      dx = dy @ W^T
y = x + f(x)       ->   the gradient of x is the sum of the two paths
```

Hand-written gradients are easy to get wrong, so they are checked against a method that needs no calculus. Nudge one weight up and down by a tiny amount and see how the loss moves:

```text
f(w) = w^2 at w = 3       the formula says the gradient is 2w = 6
(f(3.001) - f(2.999)) / 0.002 = (9.006001 - 8.994001) / 0.002 = 6.000
```

The test does this for every weight of a tiny model in float64 and requires the two gradients of each parameter group to agree with a relative error below 0.000001.

### Adam and mini-batches

Each training step draws 32 random windows of 32 characters. The target of every position is simply the next character, so no labels are needed. The loss is measured, backpropagated, and the **Adam** optimiser moves every weight. Adam keeps two running averages per weight, of the gradient and of its square, and steps by the first divided by the square root of the second: every weight gets a step of similar size, whatever the scale of its gradient. Training takes 1000 steps, about half a minute on one CPU core.

## Results

All numbers below come from the demo and are committed in [`results/results.md`](../../../projects/artificial-intelligence/tiny-language-model/results/results.md).

| Model | Context it sees | Train loss | Held-out loss | Held-out perplexity |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Uniform guess, ln(45) | nothing |  | 3.807 | 45.00 |
| Bigram (counting) | 1 character | 1.730 | 1.741 | 5.70 |
| Transformer | up to 32 characters | 0.640 | 0.662 | 1.94 |

![Loss curve](../../../projects/artificial-intelligence/tiny-language-model/results/loss-curve.svg)

Three things to read:

1. Seeing one character takes the perplexity from 45 to 5.7. Seeing the context takes it to 1.94: on average the transformer hesitates between two characters, the bigram between almost six.
2. The transformer passes the bigram within the first 100 steps and then improves slowly.
3. Its training loss (0.640) and its held-out loss (0.662) are close, so it learned rules and did not memorise lines.

The loss cannot reach 0. The noun of a sentence or the numbers of a sum are chosen at random by the generator, and nobody can predict them.

### What the context buys

The probability each model gives to the character the grammar demands (`_` is a space):

| Context | Right next | Wrong next | Bigram: P(right) | Bigram: P(wrong) | Transformer: P(right) | Transformer: P(wrong) |
| --- | :---: | :---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| `ana_has_a_cat._` | `s` | `h` | 0.141 | 0.077 | 0.982 | 0.011 |
| `leo_has_a_cat._` | `h` | `s` | 0.077 | 0.141 | 0.980 | 0.014 |
| `the_old_dogs_see` | `_` | `s` | 0.395 | 0.099 | 0.999 | 0.000 |
| `the_old_dog_see` | `s` | `_` | 0.099 | 0.395 | 0.998 | 0.001 |
| `tom_says_4+5=` | `9` | `1` | 0.091 | 0.417 | 0.433 | 0.410 |
| `tom_says_7+8=1` | `5` | `.` | 0.051 | 0.110 | 0.350 | 0.000 |
| `{[x]` | `}` | `]` | 0.065 | 0.100 | 0.210 | 0.005 |
| `[{x}` | `]` | `}` | 0.113 | 0.099 | 0.327 | 0.009 |

In the first two rows the bigram gives exactly the same numbers for "ana" and for "leo": all it sees is the space. The transformer reads the name. After `{[x]` it gives 0.210 to `}` and only 0.005 to `]`. The rest of the probability goes to continuing with a letter or opening another bracket, which the grammar also allows.

The sums are the honest weak point: after `4+5=` the model gives 0.433 to "9" and 0.410 to "1". It learned that a digit comes next and it is only half way to learning the addition table in 1000 steps. An average loss hides this kind of detail, and probes like these show it.

## Sampling: how the next character is chosen

The trained model is fixed. What changes the style of the output is how a character is chosen from its probabilities.

| Method | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Greedy | always takes the most probable character |
| Temperature T | divides the scores by T before softmax |
| Top-k | keeps the k most probable characters and renormalises |
| Top-p (nucleus) | keeps the smallest set of most probable characters whose probabilities reach p, including the one that crosses p, and renormalises |

Variety is measured with the **entropy** of the distribution, in bits: 0 when one character has all the probability, 1 for a fair coin, log2(3) = 1.585 for three equally likely characters. For the scores (2, 1, 0):

```text
T = 0.5   (0.867, 0.117, 0.016)   entropy 0.636 bits     sharper
T = 1.0   (0.665, 0.245, 0.090)   entropy 1.201 bits     the model as trained
T = 2.0   (0.506, 0.307, 0.186)   entropy 1.472 bits     flatter
```

And for top-k and top-p on the probabilities (0.5, 0.3, 0.15, 0.05), entropy 1.648 bits:

```text
top-k 2     keeps 0.5 and 0.3                    -> (0.625, 0.375)   0.954 bits
top-p 0.7   0.5 is not enough, 0.5 + 0.3 = 0.8
            crosses 0.7, so both stay            -> (0.625, 0.375)   0.954 bits
```

The demo writes 100 lines with each setting, always from the same seed, and measures the mean entropy of the distributions the characters were really drawn from:

| Setting | Mean entropy (bits) | Distinct lines of 100 | Grammatical lines of 100 | First line written |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| greedy | 0.000 | 1 | 100 | `the new cat sees a cup.` |
| temperature 0.2 | 0.304 | 66 | 100 | `the old cups see a cup.` |
| temperature 0.5 | 0.520 | 97 | 95 | `the old bags see a cat.` |
| temperature 1.0 | 0.856 | 99 | 73 | `ana says 5+8=14.` |
| temperature 1.5 | 1.464 | 100 | 38 | `[9=1ups find a k map.` |
| temperature 1.0, top-k 3 | 0.380 | 86 | 85 | `leo says 4+9=14.` |
| temperature 1.0, top-p 0.9 | 0.751 | 99 | 87 | `[[x[y]{zx}x]}x` |
| temperature 1.5, top-k 3 | 0.464 | 92 | 72 | `leo says 4+9=14.` |
| temperature 1.5, top-p 0.9 | 1.039 | 99 | 67 | `[[x(y){z(yzyy)}]` |

- **Lower temperature, less variety.** The entropy falls from 1.464 to 0.856, 0.520 and 0.304 bits, and the number of different lines from 100 to 66. Greedy is the end of the road: 0 bits, and the same line 100 times.
- **Less variety, fewer mistakes.** A line is grammatical when it obeys one of the four rules. At temperature 0.2 all 100 lines do, at 1.5 only 38. A high temperature gives the unlikely characters a real chance, and one wrong character breaks a line.
- **Top-k and top-p cut the tail.** At temperature 1.5 they bring the grammatical lines from 38 back to 72 and 67 while still writing more than 90 different lines. That is why real systems combine a temperature with one of them.

For comparison, the bigram table writes lines such as `likeog w cu2+8=6it.`. None of its 100 lines is grammatical.

## What a real system adds

- **Size.** Billions of parameters, dozens of blocks, contexts of thousands of tokens, and training text measured in terabytes, on GPUs. The algorithm is the one shown here.
- **Subword tokens** in place of characters, as in [bpe-tokenizer](bpe-tokenizer.md).
- **Automatic differentiation.** Nobody writes the backward pass by hand: a framework derives it from the forward pass.
- **A key-value cache.** This demo runs the whole window again for every new character. Real systems keep the keys and values already computed.
- **Training tricks**: dropout, weight decay, learning-rate warm-up, gradient clipping, GELU in place of ReLU.
- **Fine-tuning** on conversations and instructions, which turns a next-token predictor into an assistant.
